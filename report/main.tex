\section{Abstract}
Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) is a major health risk in Beijing. Its concentrations are influenced not only by current weather conditions but also by how these conditions change over time. Prior studies often add pollution history, nearby station or forecasts, leaving unclear how much weather history contributes and whether gains hold in a later year or during severe pollution. We examine how much recent weather history and tuned tree ensembles improve same-hour PM2.5 estimation in Beijing. The UCI dataset contains 43,824 hourly records from 2010--2014, of which 41,757 have PM2.5 readings. We train on 2010--2012, tune on 2013, and evaluate models refitted on 2010--2013 against 2014 measurements. Across three tuned ensembles, adding 13 weather-history features reduced 2014 RMSE by 5.02--7.40 \conc{}. XGBoost had the lowest 2014 RMSE (60.110 \conc{}, compared with 71.397 for the baseline random forest). A paired block bootstrap showed that the RMSE difference between the two boosted models was uncertain. All models underestimated high-pollution hours ($\geq$221 \conc{}, the top 10\%) on average, including XGBoost, whose signed error averaged -117.7 \conc{} in that group. Weather history improved overall estimation, but large errors remain when pollution is most severe.

\section{Introduction}
Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) consists of particles with aerodynamic diameters of about 2.5 micrometers or less. Long-term exposure to fine particulate pollution is associated with adverse health outcomes, including cardiopulmonary mortality \citep{pope2002}. Beijing has experienced severe PM2.5 episodes in which meteorological conditions play a major role \citep{liang2015}. Weather affects the conditions under which particulate pollution can accumulate or disperse. A model based on routinely measured weather can help characterize how much of the variation in hourly concentrations is predictable from those conditions and where such estimates remain unreliable.

Weather forecasts, local monitoring stations and pollution data are frequently used in machine-learning studies of urban air quality\citep{zheng2015}. Although these additional inputs can increase accuracy, they make it challenging to determine how much the recent weather history alone contributes. Furthermore, a model's performance in a subsequent year or during the most polluted hours can be concealed by a single total error score.

We ask how much historical meteorological feature engineering and optimized ensemble learning improve hourly PM2.5 estimation under a chronological evaluation protocol. We specifically address three research questions: \begin{enumerate} 
\item[\textbf{RQ1}] When hyperparameters are held fixed, does adding recent weather history improve estimation? 
\item[\textbf{RQ2}]Do tuned tree ensembles outperform the original baselines on a later, held-out year, and are the differences between them statistically reliable?
\item[\textbf{RQ3}]Where do the models remain unreliable, particularly during high-pollution hours and across seasons?
\end{enumerate}

The inputs include weather observed in the same hour as the pollution measurement, so the task is contemporaneous estimation. A future-hour forecast would require a defined lead time and predictors available at the time the forecast is issued.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. We first review related work and identify the research gap, then state our contributions. Next, we describe the methodology, dataset, and experimental setup, and present results for each research question. Finally, we discuss limitations and conclude with directions for future research.

\section{Related Work and Research Gap}
\paragraph{Weather and PM2.5 in Beijing.}
\citet{liang2015} used meteorological information to adjust Beijing pollution measurements and examine changes associated with emissions controls and winter heating. Our inclusion of weather-history elements is motivated by their study, which demonstrates the relevance of both past and present weather conditions to PM2.5. However, rather than assessing how well a model predicts specific hourly concentrations, their objective was weather adjustment for policy assessment. 

\paragraph{Prediction with pollution history and same-hour estimation.} Many PM2.5 prediction studies combine weather data with historical pollution measurements. \citet{zheng2015} used temporal patterns, nearby station readings, and weather forecasts to predict air quality up to 48 hours ahead. \citet{li2017} and \citet{huang2018} applied LSTM and CNN--LSTM models in Beijing, while \citet{joharestani2019} found that historical PM2.5 measurements improved prediction accuracy using XGBoost. Unlike forecasting, same-hour estimation focuses on current concentrations. \citet{zheng2013} estimated air quality using meteorological and urban data, while \citet{wei2019} applied a space-time Random Forest across China. More recently, \citet{wang2026} achieved $R^2=0.977$ for same-hour estimation, but PM2.5 history accounted for approximately 75\% of XGBoost's feature gain. These findings leave the independent contribution of weather history unclear.
\paragraph{Learning algorithms.}
Ridge regression shrinks coefficients in correlated designs \citep{hoerl1970}. Random forests average trees built using resampled data and randomized feature selection \citep{breiman2001}. Gradient boosting fits an additive model in stages using the gradient of a loss function \citep{friedman2001}, and XGBoost extends this approach with regularized tree learning \citep{chen2016}. We use these established algorithms and focus on the experimental design around them.
\paragraph{Evaluation of time-ordered data.}
Temporal dependence requires careful out-of-sample evaluation \citep{tashman2000,bergmeir2012}. However, studies such as \citet{garg2021} report overall error metrics without uncertainty intervals or feature ablation. Block bootstrap resampling preserves local temporal dependence when estimating uncertainty in model performance differences \citep{kunsch1989}.
\paragraph{Research gap.}
Based on the reviewed literature, this study examines whether weather history improves estimation without pollution measurements (RQ1), whether observed performance differences remain reliable after accounting for temporal dependence (RQ2), and whether overall metrics hide estimation errors during high-pollution hours or particular seasons (RQ3).



\section{Novelty and Research Contributions}
The originality of this study lies in its controlled experimental design rather than the development of a new learning algorithm. We compare seven model configurations using identical chronological splits. For each of the three tree ensembles, a $2\times2$ ablation separates the effects of historical weather features and hyperparameter tuning (RQ1). Paired block-bootstrap intervals assess the reliability of observed RMSE differences between models (RQ2). We further examine permutation importance and errors across pollution levels and seasons to identify limitations that overall performance metrics may hide (RQ3). Together, these experiments provide a clearer understanding of the benefits and limitations of PM2.5 estimation using meteorological and calendar information alone.

\section{Proposed Methodology}
\subsection{Data preparation and weather history}
We parse the date fields, sort rows by timestamp and verify that the raw series has one row per hour. Seasons follow calendar months: December--February is winter, March--May is spring, June--August is summer, and September--November is autumn. The ten original predictors are temperature (\texttt{TEMP}), dew point (\texttt{DEWP}), pressure (\texttt{PRES}), cumulative wind speed (\texttt{Iws}), accumulated snow hours (\texttt{Is}), accumulated rain hours (\texttt{Ir}), hour, month, combined wind direction (\texttt{cbwd}), and season. Temperature and dew point are in degrees Celsius and pressure is reported in hPa. Season and wind direction are categorical. The row ID, year, day of month, and all current or past PM2.5 values are excluded from the input.

For each of \texttt{DEWP}, \texttt{PRES}, \texttt{TEMP}, and \texttt{Iws}, we compute a three-hour difference and two trailing means. With $t=1$ at the beginning of the record and $m=\min(t,w)$,
\begin{equation}
\Delta_3 x_t=x_t-x_{t-3}, \qquad
\bar x_{t,w}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}x_{t-j}, \quad w\in\{24,72\}.
\label{eq:history}
\end{equation}
The last additional feature sums recorded \texttt{Ir} values over the current and prior 23 hours, or the available prefix at the start. These operations use the full hourly weather series before rows with missing targets are removed, so a missing pollution reading does not compress the time window. The change in \texttt{Iws} describes a difference in a cumulative series, which can reflect resets as well as accumulation. The \texttt{Ir} sum can count accumulated rain hours repeatedly and serves as a rain-history proxy. It has no interpretation as rainfall depth, volume, or distinct hours of rain. Together these transformations add 13 inputs before encoding.

Numeric gaps are filled with training medians and categorical gaps with training modes. Wind direction and season are one-hot encoded using categories observed at fit time. We retain numeric standardization for the original pipelines and use unscaled inputs for the new tree pipelines. Each preprocessing stage is fitted on the corresponding training period. Unavailable three-hour differences at the very start of the raw series occur on rows with missing targets.

\subsection{Regression models and objectives}
The mean baseline predicts the mean measured concentration in its training data. Linear Regression predicts $\hat y_i=\beta_0+\boldsymbol x_i^\top\boldsymbol\beta$ and fits coefficients by minimizing $\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2$. Ridge adds $\alpha\|\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2$ with $\alpha=1$ to shrink coefficients, leaving the intercept unpenalized.

A forest predicts $B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}T_b(\boldsymbol x)$, where each regression tree $T_b$ is fitted to a bootstrap sample. A smaller feature fraction limits the candidate variables at each split. The original forest uses 100 trees, all candidate features, unrestricted depth, and a minimum of two samples per leaf. The tuned forest selects these settings from its own grid.

For boosting, the model score after step $m$ is $F_m(\boldsymbol x)=F_{m-1}(\boldsymbol x)+\eta T_m(\boldsymbol x)$. Each tree updates the score in a direction that reduces the chosen loss, with learning rate $\eta$ controlling the update size. Both implementations use binned inputs when searching for splits. XGBoost also penalizes tree complexity and uses second-order information to choose splits and leaf weights. Given an observation $y$ and a predicted mean $\mu$, the explored losses, up to constants and positive scale factors, are
\begin{equation}
\ell_{\rm SE}=(y-\mu)^2,\qquad
\ell_{\rm P}=\mu-y\log\mu,\qquad
\ell_{\rm T}=\frac{\mu^{2-p}}{2-p}-\frac{y\mu^{1-p}}{1-p}.
\label{eq:losses}
\end{equation}
Squared error uses $\mu=F_m$. Poisson and Tweedie use $\mu=\exp(F_m)$ and thus produce positive estimates while allowing zero targets. Their losses weight errors differently across the concentration range. The Tweedie variance power is $p=1.5$, which corresponds to a variance proportional to $\mu^{1.5}$ under that working model. We compare these objectives because the concentrations are nonnegative and right-skewed. PM2.5 is a mass concentration, and the experiment does not test a distributional model for it.

\subsection{Selection and scoring}
We train on 2010--2012 and score each candidate on 2013. The lowest validation RMSE selects the settings for each ensemble. During the XGBoost search, the 2013 RMSE is monitored after every tree and training stops if it has failed to improve for 100 rounds, up to a maximum of 3,000 trees. The saved tree count includes the best round. The final fits start fresh on 2010--2013, with that tree count fixed and early stopping disabled. We use no shuffled folds.

For $n$ labeled evaluation observations and evaluation mean $\bar y$, the measures are
\begin{align}
\mathrm{MAE} &= n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i-\hat y_i|,\\
\mathrm{RMSE} &= \sqrt{n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2},\\
R^2 &= 1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat y_i)^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
\end{align}
MAE is the average error magnitude. Squaring the errors makes RMSE more sensitive to large misses. The $R^2$ reference mean comes from the evaluation period, so the training-mean baseline can have a negative score. All models are evaluated on the same labeled observations in each period.

\section{Dataset and Experimental Setup}
We use the UCI Beijing PM2.5 dataset \citep{uci381}. It pairs hourly PM2.5 measurements from the U.S. Embassy in Beijing with meteorological observations from Beijing Capital International Airport. The raw file has 43,824 hourly rows and 13 columns. Only the target has missing entries. There are 2,067 missing measurements, leaving 41,757 labeled rows with a median PM2.5 of 72 \conc{} and a range of 0--994 \conc{}. Table~\ref{tab:splits} lists the chronological partitions.

All searches use the expanded features (Table~\ref{tab:grid}). Both forests use squared-error splits and bootstrap samples of the training set size. Histogram boosting uses up to 255 bins, no $L_2$ regularization and no early stopping. XGBoost uses the CPU histogram method with up to 256 bins, learning rate 0.03, $L_2$ penalty 1, $L_1$ penalty 0, and split penalty $\gamma=0$. Minimum child weight in XGBoost is a threshold on the sum of second derivatives in a leaf. The Poisson objective uses XGBoost's default maximum delta step of 0.7.

Each ablation compares inputs at fixed settings. The untuned controls use the earlier standalone forest (200 trees, all features, unlimited depth and minimum leaf size two), default histogram boosting (squared error, learning rate 0.1, 100 iterations, 31 leaves, minimum leaf size 20, with early stopping disabled), and a 100-tree squared-error XGBoost model (depth six, child weight 1, full row and column fractions, with the shared learning rate). The tuned ablations keep the selected hyperparameters fixed, including XGBoost's tree count, when removing history.

Experiments ran on CPU using Python 3.12.16, NumPy 2.2.6, pandas 2.2.3, scikit-learn 1.6.1, XGBoost 3.4.1, SciPy 1.18.1, Matplotlib 3.10.1 and seaborn 0.13.2. Stochastic fits and analyses use seed 42. Install the pinned dependencies with \texttt{python -m pip install -r requirements.txt}, then run \texttt{python main.py --mode full --jobs 4} to repeat the searches and subsequent analyses. The \texttt{--mode saved} option reproduces the final fits and diagnostics using the saved settings and skips the grids.

\subsection{Permutation importance}
For each final model we independently shuffle one original input column at a time on the 2014 rows, leaving the other columns intact. We repeat each permutation five times and measure the mean increase in RMSE. The categorical columns are shuffled before encoding, so a category and all its encoded columns are perturbed together. This gives all model families the same scoring rule and differs from split impurity or training gain, which describe how trees were built.

Random Forest was most sensitive to the 72-hour temperature mean (RMSE increased by 26.7 \conc{}). Histogram boosting was most sensitive to the 72-hour temperature mean (RMSE increased by 40.4 \conc{}). XGBoost was most sensitive to same-hour dew point (RMSE increased by 51.5 \conc{}). These scores describe predictive use in fitted models. Related inputs can substitute for one another or produce unrealistic combinations when shuffled, and the importance rankings establish no causal relationship. The prominence of 72-hour temperature means in two of the three ensembles is consistent with the ablation results for RQ1.

\subsection{Paired block-bootstrap uncertainty}
This subsection addresses the second part of RQ2: whether differences between models are statistically reliable.
We draw contiguous moving blocks of 72 and 168 clock hours with replacement in 2014, keeping the same sampled hours for every model in a pair \citep{kunsch1989}. Missing target hours remain gaps within blocks and are excluded from error totals. Each of 2,000 replicates per block length uses enough blocks, including a shorter final block, to match the original span in clock hours. Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap} gives the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the paired RMSE differences. The intervals favor the tuned over the original forest and histogram boosting over the tuned forest for both block lengths. The XGBoost--histogram boosting intervals include zero. These intervals describe error differences conditional on the fitted models and the observed year. The analysis retains dependence within blocks but does not account for changes across years or refitting uncertainty.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Our results answer the three research questions. For RQ1, weather history improved every ensemble at fixed settings, showing that the recent evolution of the weather carries information beyond same-hour readings. For RQ2, the tree ensembles clearly outperformed the mean and linear baselines, whose unconstrained fits each produced 457 negative estimates in 2014, but the two boosted models could not be distinguished statistically. For RQ3, all models underestimated the most polluted hours and performed worst in winter. Our best $R^2$ of 0.587 is well below the 0.977 that \citet{wang2026} obtained with PM2.5 history and co-pollutants, which indicates how much of the variation weather alone cannot explain.

Several limitations apply. Settings were selected on a single year (2013), which XGBoost also used for early stopping, so they may be tuned to that year's conditions. Because team members had seen some 2014 results in earlier versions of the project, the test comparisons should be read as retrospective. The data pair one embassy monitor with airport weather, omit emissions, traffic, and neighboring pollution, and exclude hours with missing readings. Accumulated weather fields can also reset, which can distort the history features. Results from one city and period may not transfer to other sites or to present-day Beijing, and because the inputs are same-hour weather, the models cannot serve as forecasts without redesigning the task.


\section{Conclusion and Future Research Directions}
This study asked how much weather history and tuned tree ensembles improve same-hour PM2.5 estimation in Beijing under chronological evaluation. For RQ1, adding 24- and 72-hour weather summaries and three-hour differences reduced 2014 RMSE by 5.02--7.40 \conc{} across three tuned ensembles at fixed settings. For RQ2, XGBoost achieved the lowest 2014 RMSE (60.110 \conc{}, $R^2=0.587$), 15.8\% below the original forest, although its advantage over histogram gradient boosting was not statistically reliable. For RQ3, all models underestimated high-pollution hours, with XGBoost underestimating 94.1\% of them, and every model had its highest error in winter. Weather history makes weather-only estimation more accurate on average, but not yet reliable when pollution is most severe.


Future work could define a one-hour-ahead target, compare against a persistence baseline, and restrict inputs to readings or weather forecasts available at issuance. Repeated chronological backtests and additional cities would help assess transfer across time and place. Adding recent pollution observations and information on traffic or emissions could test whether those inputs improve performance during high-pollution episodes. Prediction intervals could help communicate the uncertainty that remains in any individual hourly estimate.
